# Brun's theorem, machine-checked

*A formalization of the convergence of the sum of reciprocals of twin
primes in Lean 4 / mathlib, completed 2026-07-07 — and an experience
report on doing it with a tiered cascade of language models.*

---

**The result.** `Salt.N6.N6_2 : BrunStatement`, where

```lean
def BrunStatement : Prop :=
  Summable (Set.indicator {p : ℕ | p.Prime ∧ (p + 2).Prime} fun n => (1 : ℝ) / n)
```

— the sum of `1/p` over primes `p` with `p + 2` also prime converges.
Sorry-free, kernel-checked, axioms exactly mathlib's standard three
(`propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`). As a corollary,
`brunConstant` (the value of the series) is well-defined and provably
positive.

**The numbers.** 27 blueprint nodes, all proved. 2,921 lines of Lean
across 15 files, 163 declarations, plus ~1,300 lines of living
documentation. Under 24 hours from empty repository to theorem; 8h12m
from the first proof commit (07:23) to blueprint completion (15:35),
in one continuous session. Four model tiers (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, Fable)
routed by a pre-declared difficulty classification; roughly 1.3M
subagent tokens across the six delegated implement-and-verify workflows,
plus the undelegated main-loop work.

**To verify it yourself** (trust base: the ~30 lines of definitions in
`Salt/Brun.lean`, the Lean kernel, and mathlib):

```sh
lake build            # kernel-checks everything
# then, in any file:  #print axioms Salt.N6.N6_2
#   → [propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound]
```

To our knowledge (per the project's July 2026 survey), this is the first
completed formalization of Brun's theorem in a major proof assistant.
Mellendijk's Lean 4 sieve repository — whose Selberg engine we port and
credit, see Part II — names it as its end goal, but its twin-prime file
is a stub.

## Part I — The mathematics, for a reader who doesn't read Lean

### 1. The statement

Brun (1919): the sum of reciprocals of twin primes converges — in
pointed contrast to the sum over *all* primes, which diverges (Euler;
in mathlib as `not_summable_one_div_on_primes`). Convergence follows
from a counting bound: if π₂(N) denotes the number of twin-prime pairs
up to N, then

> **π₂(N) = O(N / (log N)²),**

and partial summation converts any such bound into convergence of
Σ 1/p. Both the counting bound (`TwinCountingBigO`) and the convergence
(`BrunStatement`) are formalized; the constant in the O is deliberately
crude (25,700, effective beyond a threshold near 2^100 — see §3; the
Big-O statement is eventual, so no small-N case analysis is needed).

### 2. The route: Selberg's Λ² sieve

Fix N and a threshold z. For n ∈ [1, N] form the products n(n+2). If
n > z is a twin-prime leader, n(n+2) has no prime factor ≤ z; so twin
primes in (z, N] are counted by the integers surviving sifting by all
primes ≤ z, and twins below z cost only +z. Selberg's method bounds the
sifted count: for weights λ supported on divisors d of P = ∏_{p ≤ z} p
with d² ≤ y and λ₁ = 1, the square (Σ_{d | gcd(m,P)} λ_d)² dominates
the indicator of "m coprime to P", and minimizing the resulting
quadratic form gives

> π₂(N) ≤ N/S + Σ_{d | P, d ≤ y} 3^ω(d) |R_d| + z + O(1),

where S = Σ_{ℓ | P, ℓ² ≤ y} g(ℓ) with g(ℓ) = ∏_{p | ℓ} ν(p)/(1 − ν(p)),
ν(d) = ρ(d)/d, and ρ(d) counts roots of n(n+2) ≡ 0 mod d (so ρ(2) = 1,
ρ(p) = 2 for odd p). R_d is the error in counting multiples of d among
the n(n+2), and |R_d| ≤ ρ(d) — one deviation per residue class.

Everything then rests on two estimates: **S grows** (at least a
constant times (log z)²) and **the error sum is small** (any power
saving suffices). With y = N^{1/5} and z = N^{1/10}, the error term is
≤ N^{4/5} and the main term N/S ≤ C·N/(log N)² — done.

### 3. Where the formal proof leaves the textbook

The interesting mathematical content of the formalization is not the
sieve — it's what we did when mathlib lacked the standard ingredients.
A formal proof optimizes a different cost function than a textbook one:
a step is "cheap" if the library supports it, regardless of how
elementary it looks on paper.

**Mertens-free main term.** Every textbook proof of S ≫ (log z)² goes
through Mertens' theorems. Mathlib has none of them (as of July 2026:
only the qualitative divergence of Σ 1/p). Rather than formalize
Mertens first — a bigger project than the rest of the track combined —
the proof goes through the divisor function:

1. Expand each (1 − ν(p))⁻¹ as a finite geometric series and group
   integers by radical: S dominates Σ_{m < z, m odd} ν\*(m), where
   ν\*(∏ p^a) = ∏ ν(p)^a, i.e. ν\*(m) = 2^Ω(m)/m for odd m.
2. Since τ(m) ≤ 2^Ω(m), this dominates Σ_{m < z, odd} τ(m)/m.
3. A bare-hands **divisor pairing** — every pair (a, b) of odd numbers
   below √z contributes the distinct divisor-pair (ab, a) — gives
   Σ τ(m)/m ≥ (Σ_{a < √z, odd} 1/a)².
4. Mathlib's harmonic-number bounds give the odd harmonic sum
   ≥ (log √z)/2 − C, and squaring lands on (1/64)(log z)² for z ≥ 100.

The only analytic input is `Mathlib/NumberTheory/Harmonic/`. Steps 1–4
are elementary but, as far as we know, this exact route is not written
down anywhere — it was designed for this formalization because it was
the cheapest path through the library that exists.

**Crude error term.** The sharp bound on Σ 3^ω(d)|R_d| uses divisor-sum
asymptotics — also absent. But any power saving works: |R_d| ≤ ρ(d) ≤
2^ω(d), so each term is ≤ 6^ω(d) ≤ d³ for squarefree d, and the whole
sum is ≤ y⁴. With y = N^{1/5} that's N^{4/5}, negligible against
N/(log N)², with exponent room to spare (any y = N^θ, θ < 1/4, works).

**Loose constants throughout.** The final bound is
π₂(N) ≤ 25,700·N/(log N)² for all N beyond a threshold on the order
of 2^100. No constant in the development is tight, by policy: every target theorem is existential or asymptotic, so tightness
buys nothing, and every hour spent on a sharp constant is an hour of
pure waste. Where a bound needed a number, the proof takes a generous
round one (256·N^{4/5} rather than N^{4/5}; threshold 2^100 chosen
*because* log(2^100) = 100·log 2 is exact and needs no decimal
estimates).

### 4. The proof chain, in fifteen statements

| # | Step | Plain statement |
|---|------|-----------------|
| 1 | `rho` | ρ(d) = #{n mod d : n(n+2) ≡ 0}, the local density. |
| 2 | `rho_mul_of_coprime` | ρ is multiplicative (CRT). |
| 3 | `progression_count_bound` | #{n ≤ N : d \| n(n+2)} = N·ρ(d)/d ± ρ(d). |
| 4 | `nu`, `sieve` | ν = ρ(d)/d packaged as a mathlib `BoundingSieve` on the values n(n+2), n ∈ [1,N], sifted by primes dividing a squarefree P. |
| 5 | `twin_count_le_siftedSum` | Twin primes in (z, N] are counted by the sifted sum when all prime factors of P are ≤ z. |
| 6 | `selberg_bound_simple` | Selberg's fundamental theorem: siftedSum ≤ N/S + Σ_{d ≤ y} 3^ω(d)\|R_d\|. *(Ported — see Part II.)* |
| 7 | `divSum_ge_sq` | The divisor pairing: Σ_{m ∈ S} τ(m)/m ≥ (Σ_{a ∈ T} 1/a)². |
| 8 | `nuStar_sum_le_gTwin_sum` | The geometric expansion: Σ_{m<z odd} 2^Ω(m)/m ≤ Σ_{ℓ<z odd sqfree} g(ℓ). |
| 9 | `exists_const_mainTermSum_ge` | The main term: Σ g(ℓ) ≥ (1/64)(log z)² for z ≥ 100. |
| 10 | `N4_2` | The error sum: Σ_{d<y sqfree} 3^ω(d)\|R_d\| ≤ y⁴. |
| 11 | `N5_2` | Assembled: π₂(N) ≤ N/S + 256·N^{4/5} + (N^{1/10}+1), with y = N^{1/5}, P = primorial(⌊N^{1/10}⌋). |
| 12 | `N5_3` | Absorbed: π₂(N) = O(N/(log N)²). |
| 13 | `integrableAtFilter_inv_id_mul_log_sq` | 1/(t(log t)²) is integrable at ∞ (antiderivative −1/log t). |
| 14 | `N6_2` | Abel summation (mathlib's `summable_mul_of_bigO_atTop'`) with f = 1/t, coefficients the twin indicator, dominator 1/(t(log t)²): **Brun's theorem**. |
| 15 | `brunConstant_pos` | Brun's constant = Σ' of the series, positive (the pair (3,5) contributes 1/3). |

### 5. What a skeptical reader should check

Machine-checking moves the trust question: not "is each step right?"
(the kernel guarantees that) but "do the definitions say what's
claimed?" The definitions to read are ~30 lines in `Salt/Brun.lean`:
`twinPrimeCounting` (a `Nat.count` of the twin predicate),
`TwinCountingBigO`, and `BrunStatement` (quoted above). If those say
what you think Brun's theorem says, the rest is the kernel's problem.
The axiom audit (`#print axioms`) confirms no `sorry`, no
`native_decide`, no custom axioms — only propositional extensionality,
choice, and quotient soundness, the standard classical base of mathlib.

## Part II — The experience, for the formalization community

### 6. Setup: blueprint first, tiers second

The project was structured before any proof was attempted:

- **A blueprint** (`docs/blueprints/brun.md`): 27 nodes in a dependency
  DAG, each with a formal statement and a difficulty class assigned in
  advance — **A** (one-step/computational), **B** (standard multi-step,
  2–5 library lemmas), **C** (real proof design), **D** (open-ended;
  never attempted by automation).
- **A model cascade**: Haiku may attempt A; Sonnet A–B; Opus A–C; Fable
  (the frontier tier) anything, plus the exclusive right to change
  statements or design. Iron rules: never alter a blueprint statement
  to make a proof go through; no `sorry` on the track branch; at most
  3 serious attempts per node, then flag loudly and move on; every
  landing updates the docs in the same commit.
- **A living guide** (`docs/blueprints/brun-guide.md`) with per-node
  cards, Mermaid DAGs, and a briefing block, kept consistent by a lint
  script that also re-audits the axioms of every cited declaration; and
  an append-only session log (`flags.md`) recording every attempt,
  failure, and library gotcha.

### 7. What the cascade actually did

Final attribution of the 27 nodes by executing tier:

| Tier | Nodes landed | Notes |
|------|--------------|-------|
| Haiku | 2 | ρ's definition, strict monotonicity of n(n+2). Plus one *useful failure* (below). |
| Sonnet | 14 | The workhorse: all of M2's number theory, the M3 estimates, and — driving delegated implement+verify workflows — the entire assembly spine N3.6/N5.2/N5.3/N6.2 and the final N6.3. |
| Opus | 8 | The C-class nodes: the delegated M1 port, the ν\* expansion (N3.2), the sieve instance, both escalation rescues. |
| Fable | 3 | Scaffold/targets, the first port node (N1.1), all statement amendments and design decisions. |

**Escalations: four, of which two were phantoms.** The cascade's
"give up early, loudly" rule was tested four times. N4.1 (Haiku →
Sonnet): real — the "one-liner" 6^ω ≤ d³ needs the squarefree
product API. N6.1 (Sonnet → Opus): the Sonnet flag predicted a hard
change-of-variables integration; Opus found the substitution was a red
herring (the antiderivative −1/log t needs none). N2.6 (Sonnet declined
→ Opus): the feared "choose the sieve level z" design decision
evaporated — z enters only through a parameter that later nodes fix.
M1 (Opus declined → Fable): genuine — mathlib recon confirmed the
Selberg optimal-weight machinery simply isn't in the library, and the
node became a port (below). The two phantom escalations cost minutes
each; that's the flag-don't-grind rule working as designed. The two
real ones routed hard work to the right tier.

**The difficulty predictions were systematically optimistic.** Five
nodes came in harder than predicted (N2.4 and N3.4 at ~C versus
predicted B, N4.1 at B versus A, N4.2 mildly, N5.2 at C versus B) —
and **zero came in easier**. The blueprint never once overestimated a
node. If you pre-classify a formalization DAG, budget for one-sided
error.

### 8. The port: standing on Mellendijk's work

The Selberg fundamental theorem (M1, nodes N1.2–N1.4) was not proved
from scratch. Mathlib's `SelbergSieve.lean` — itself the upstreamed
foundation of Mellendijk's `selberg-sieve4` repository (Apache-2.0) —
provides the Λ² upper bound and the main-sum diagonalization but stops
before the optimal-weight choice and the mainSum = 1/S identity.
Those ~440 lines were ported from the repository's `Selberg.lean` onto
current mathlib, with attribution. The ~2.5 years of API drift
(Lean v4.7.0-rc2 → v4.32.0-rc1) was entirely mechanical: renames,
a deprecated `conv` idiom, one deliberate refactor of an S⁻¹²
factoring. One statement amendment was made at the Fable tier before
porting (truncation boundaries d < √y → d² ≤ y) to match the
reference's convention exactly — mathematically immaterial (g > 0),
and recorded in the log, but the kind of thing worth deciding *before*
a delegated agent hits a false statement.

The genuinely new formal content is everything else: the twin
instantiation (M2), the Mertens-free main term (M3, including the
divisor-pairing and geometric-expansion arguments designed for this
proof), the crude error bound (M4), and the assembly spine (M5–M6).

### 9. The verification discipline

Every delegated node went through three independent layers:

1. **An implementation agent** working from a design brief. The
   driver worked out the mathematics first — the full inequality
   chain, the crux library lemmas (pre-validated in isolation by
   compiling them in a scratch file *before* writing the brief), and
   explicit license to loosen any constant. Briefs forbade `sorry`
   categorically: a stuck agent must comment out the dependent theorem
   and leave a precise statement of what remains ("PORT-BLOCKER"),
   keeping everything else compiling. (This clause was never
   triggered.)
2. **An adversarial verification agent**, given the implementer's
   report but instructed not to trust it: re-run the build, re-grep
   for forbidden constructs, re-derive the axiom audit, quote the
   final theorem statement character-for-character, and check the
   proof is not vacuous.
3. **The driver's own third pass**: build, sweep, read the entire
   file line by line, run its own `#print axioms`.

For the final theorem this was tightened further: the verifier
additionally confirmed via `git log`/`git diff` that `Salt/Brun.lean` —
where `BrunStatement` lives — was never modified by any proving
session, and traced that the proof genuinely routes through the
twin-density bound (a bare `Summable`-of-1/n claim is *false* in
general, so a shortcut would have had to be a soundness bug, but the
check costs little and the failure mode it targets — proving a
look-alike — is the one the kernel can't catch).

Is three layers overkill when the kernel checks everything anyway?
For *correctness*, yes. The layers exist for the two things the kernel
does not check: that the proved statement is the intended one, and
that the proof is honest work rather than an accidental vacuity
(a hypothesis never satisfiable, a constant that trivializes the
claim). Both failure modes were live concerns with delegated
generation; neither occurred, but the N5.3 near-miss (§10) justified
the paranoia.

### 10. What broke, and what it taught

**A false inequality in a design brief.** The N5.3 brief suggested a
concrete numeric threshold for a log estimate. The implementing agent
found the suggested inequality was *genuinely false*, abandoned it, and
chose N₀ = 2^100 instead — a power of two, so log N₀ = 100·log 2 is
exact and no decimal estimates are needed. Two lessons: briefs should
suggest shapes, not numbers, and "full freedom to loosen constants"
must be explicit or an agent may grind against the brief's own error.

**Definitional equality is a landmine for the smallest models.**
Haiku's ρ definition used a `d+1` pattern match; that made `Fintype
(ZMod d)` instances fail to unify when Sonnet later rewrote p =
(p−1)+1 — syntactically different `NeZero` proofs give non-defeq
instances. The fix (an `if h : d = 0` form) is standard lore, but it
means A-tier definitional work can silently tax B-tier proving work.

**Junk values make true statements false at the edge.** The odd
harmonic bound is *false* at n = 1: (1/2 : ℕ) = 0 and mathlib's
log 0 = 0 conspire to break the general monotonicity argument. Split
base cases, don't smooth.

**Phantom difficulty is real and cheap to dispel.** Twice a tier flag
predicted hard analysis (a substitution, a design decision) that a
higher tier dissolved in one attempt. The asymmetry — minutes to flag,
minutes for the higher tier to check — is the strongest argument for
the cascade over a monolithic strong model: the flags were wrong about
the *difficulty* but right to be cheap about it.

**Docs rot silently; assert your automation.** During report
preparation, an extraction agent caught three stale status markers in
the guide: scripted string replacements in earlier commits had guessed
strings that didn't match the actual text and no-opped without error.
The lint audited cards but not the graphs and tables, so it stayed
green. (One card was also stale in the other direction: N3.3's second
half had been proved en passant inside N3.6 and never marked.) Fixes:
replacement scripts now assert on hit-count, and the lint should
cross-check status tokens across cards, graphs, and tables — docs
automation needs the same paranoia as proof automation.

**A library-gotcha log pays compound interest.** Twenty-odd mathlib
trench notes accumulated in `flags.md` (namespace surprises, parser
precedence with `if-then-else` inside sums swallowing a trailing `≤`,
`tendsto_nat_floor_atTop` needing an explicit type ascription, …).
Later briefs quoted relevant entries, and later nodes demonstrably
avoided repeat losses — the recon habit of confirming a lemma's exact
namespace *before* delegating saved at least one full iteration per
assembly node.

### 11. Doctrine that earned its keep

- **Classify, then prove; never reclassify to justify grinding.** The
  3-attempt budget plus mandatory flagging converted every stall into
  either a cheap escalation or a recorded fact.
- **Loosen every constant, everywhere, explicitly.** Not one hour of
  this track was spent on a tight bound. The final 25,700 is absurd
  and no one cares.
- **Defer parameters.** The sieve was built over *any* squarefree P
  with the level a free parameter; the twin-specific choices
  (primorial, N^{1/5}) enter only at assembly. Both feared design
  nodes (N2.6, N5.1) deflated on contact with this pattern.
- **Pre-validate the crux.** Before writing a delegation brief, compile
  the two or three make-or-break library invocations in a scratch
  file. Every brief whose crux was pre-validated came back complete.
- **Statements are sacred, and someone must own them.** The one
  statement change of the track went through the designated
  highest-tier escape hatch and is logged. Everything below that tier
  proves what's written or flags — which is precisely what makes
  27 machine-generated proofs trustworthy as *the theorem* rather
  than as 27 theorems-shaped objects.

### 13. What's next

Three pieces are candidates for mathlib upstreaming: the completed
Selberg fundamental theorem (closing the gap mathlib's own
`SelbergSieve.lean` leaves open), the Mertens-free main-term bound,
and the congruence-counting block-decomposition module. The project's
stated ladder continues toward Bombieri–Vinogradov and Maynard–Tao —
each rung a real theorem, each a test of whether this discipline
scales past a one-day track.
